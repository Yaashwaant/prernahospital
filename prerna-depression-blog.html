What Is Depression?

Depression — clinically known as Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) — is a serious medical condition that affects how a person thinks, feels, and functions in daily life. It is not a sign of weakness, laziness, or a bad attitude. It is a neurological illness rooted in brain chemistry, genetics, life experiences, and psychological factors.

Unlike ordinary sadness that fades after a bad week, clinical depression persists for at least two weeks or longer and significantly impairs a person's ability to work, maintain relationships, and carry out basic self-care.

Common Symptoms of Depression

1

Persistent Sadness or Emptiness

A prolonged feeling of deep sadness, hopelessness, or emotional emptiness that simply doesn't lift. This isn't the typical low mood after disappointment — it's a heavy, lingering weight. You may find yourself crying for no apparent reason or feeling that nothing brings joy anymore.

Don't ignore it if: the sadness has lasted more than two weeks with no clear cause.
2

Loss of Interest in Activities You Once Loved

A hallmark symptom called anhedonia — the inability to feel pleasure. Hobbies, social outings, sports, food, or intimacy that once brought happiness suddenly feel pointless or exhausting. If you've stopped doing the things that used to light you up, your mind is sending you a signal worth listening to.

Don't ignore it if: withdrawal from enjoyable activities has lasted weeks without explanation.
3

Constant Fatigue and Low Energy

Feeling physically and mentally drained — even after a full night's sleep. This isn't ordinary tiredness. Patients describe it as carrying an invisible weight that makes even simple tasks like bathing, cooking, or answering a phone call feel overwhelming.

Don't ignore it if: exhaustion is constant and unexplained by physical illness or activity.
4

Changes in Sleep Patterns

Depression wreaks havoc on sleep. Some people sleep far too much (hypersomnia) and still feel exhausted. Others experience severe insomnia — lying awake for hours, waking at 3 a.m. with racing thoughts, or waking earlier than needed and being unable to fall back asleep.

Don't ignore it if: your sleep has shifted dramatically and persists for weeks.
5

Significant Changes in Appetite or Weight

A sudden loss of appetite — or the opposite, emotional overeating — can both signal depression. Many patients experience noticeable weight loss or gain over a short period. The body and the mind are deeply connected, and what's happening emotionally almost always shows up physically.

Don't ignore it if: appetite or weight has changed significantly without dieting or illness.
6

Difficulty Concentrating, Remembering, or Making Decisions

"Brain fog" is a common complaint among people with depression. You may struggle to focus at work, forget conversations, or feel paralysed when faced with even minor decisions. This cognitive impairment is a direct result of how depression affects neural pathways in the brain — and it is not "just stress."

Don't ignore it if: cognitive slowing is affecting your work or everyday decisions.
7

Feelings of Worthlessness or Excessive Guilt

People with depression often develop an intensely negative inner narrative. They hold themselves responsible for things beyond their control, feel like a burden to loved ones, or believe they are fundamentally flawed or undeserving of happiness. These thought patterns are a symptom of illness — not a reflection of reality.

Don't ignore it if: negative self-talk has become relentless or irrational.
8

Unexplained Physical Aches and Pains

Depression frequently manifests as physical complaints: chronic headaches, backaches, stomach pain, or digestive issues that have no clear medical cause. When the mind is in distress, the body keeps score. If you've been to multiple doctors for physical pain and found no explanation, consider consulting a psychiatrist.

Don't ignore it if: physical pain has no medical explanation after thorough testing.
9

Irritability, Restlessness, or Agitation

Depression doesn't always look like sadness. In many patients — especially men and adolescents — it presents as frustration, anger, irritability, or a hair-trigger temper. You may feel restless, on edge, or snapping at people without understanding why. This "angry depression" is real and just as serious as the tearful kind.

Don't ignore it if: irritability feels out of proportion to events and has lasted weeks.
10

Thoughts of Death or Suicide

This is the symptom that must never be dismissed. If you or someone you love is experiencing recurring thoughts of death, self-harm, or suicide — even vague thoughts like "I wish I wasn't here" — please seek help immediately. These thoughts are a medical emergency, and compassionate, effective help is available.

When Should You See a Psychiatrist?

Many people delay seeking help because they hope things will improve on their own — or because they fear judgment. Here's a simple rule: if your symptoms have lasted more than two weeks and are affecting your ability to function, it's time to see a doctor.

  • You experience five or more of the symptoms listed above
  • You are missing work or neglecting responsibilities due to your mood
  • You have withdrawn from family and friends for weeks
  • You are using alcohol or substances to cope with how you feel
  • You have any thoughts of self-harm or suicide
